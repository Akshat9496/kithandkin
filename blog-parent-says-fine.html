NRI & Distance Families

When your parent says "I'm fine" — and you know they're not.

Kith & Kin — Elder Concierge5 min readDelhi NCR
← Back to all articles

You call on a Sunday evening. It is 9pm in London, half past one in the morning in Delhi. Your mother answers on the second ring — which is already unusual. She sounds fine. She says she is fine. She mentions nothing of consequence. The call ends in six minutes.

And yet. Something felt off. A slight hesitation before she said "I'm managing." The way she changed the subject when you asked about the doctor's appointment. The background — no television, no sounds of the house you grew up in.

You are not imagining it. And you are not alone.

The three words that haunt NRI families

"Haan beta, main theek hoon."

These words are the most commonly said — and least trusted — words in NRI family life. Research confirms what most of us already know: elderly Indian parents routinely downplay their health and circumstances to protect their children abroad from worry. It is not deception. It is love. But it leaves families 5,000 miles away trying to make important decisions from incomplete information.

The problem compounds when family members who are physically nearby also minimise things — either to avoid conflict, because they have become accustomed to gradual decline, or because they genuinely do not notice what a fresh pair of eyes would catch.

What "fine" can actually mean

In our experience visiting families across Delhi NCR, "I'm fine" has covered:

  • A fall that happened two weeks ago, mentioned only because we asked directly
  • Medication left untouched in the box because "I didn't want to worry anyone"
  • A slow withdrawal from social activities that no one had noted as significant
  • Meals being skipped because cooking felt too tiring
  • Persistent joint pain simply accepted as part of ageing, without treatment

None of these are catastrophic on their own. Together, and over time, they can become serious. And the NRI family — who spoke to their parent for six minutes on Sunday — has no way of knowing.

Why this happens

Indian parents of a certain generation were raised to endure. Complaining is not in their vocabulary. Asking for help is not in their culture. Worrying their children — especially children who sacrificed proximity for opportunity abroad — feels like an imposition.

There is also something else at play. Many elderly parents live with a quiet pride in being independent. Admitting difficulty feels like defeat. "I'm fine" is not just reassurance. It is a statement of identity.

What you can do from abroad

The honest answer is: not very much, alone. Video calls do not show the state of the kitchen. WhatsApp conversations do not reveal how your parent walks, or whether they are sleeping well, or whether their blood pressure has crept up. Your relatives who check in mean well but are not trained to notice the things that matter.

What makes a real difference is a regular, trusted, professional presence in the home. Someone who visits not just to check a list, but to notice — the quality of your parent's mood, the food in the refrigerator, the ease or difficulty of movement, the small things that add up to a full and honest picture.

"The most powerful thing is not more frequent calls. It is one person on the ground who can tell you the truth."

From a recent visit: One family told us their mother had been waking at 3am every night for six weeks. She had not mentioned it to any of her children. She told our associate in the first visit, because someone finally asked her how she was sleeping — not just how she was.
