NRI & Distance Families

How to know if your parent needs more support.

Kith & Kin — Elder Concierge5 min readDelhi NCR
← Back to all articles

There is no clean moment when it becomes obvious. There is no test you can run from a distance, no checklist your parent will complete honestly. The need for more support tends to accumulate gradually — in small changes that are easy to explain away until they are not.

This guide is for NRI families who are starting to wonder. Not panicking — just noticing. Asking themselves, on the drive home from work or in the quiet after a video call: "Are they really okay?"

Signs to watch for on video calls

Video calls are imperfect windows into your parents' lives, but they are what most NRI families have. Here is what to watch for, beyond what your parent tells you.

Their environment

  • Is the background consistently the same, limited part of the house? This can indicate reduced mobility.
  • Does the background look noticeably different — less tidy, less organised?
  • Are they in the same clothes across multiple calls?

Their affect and energy

  • Is their energy lower than usual, even accounting for the time difference?
  • Have they stopped mentioning activities they used to mention — morning walks, visiting friends, watching their cricket?
  • Do they change the subject when you ask about specific things: health, eating, sleeping?

Their memory and orientation

  • Are they repeating questions or stories within the same conversation?
  • Is there confusion about dates, appointments, or recent events that seems new?

Signs from other sources

Relatives and neighbours who are physically present have information you do not — but they may not know what to look for, or may not want to worry you. Ask direct, specific questions rather than general ones. "Has Papa been going out?" yields more than "Is everything okay?"

  • Ask about specific activities: are they going to the local temple? The market? Meeting neighbours?
  • Ask about eating: is food being cooked regularly? Are they eating alone?
  • Ask about health incidents: have there been any falls, dizzy spells, or times they seemed unwell?
  • Ask about mood: do they seem more withdrawn than usual?

The medication red flag

Medication non-compliance is the single most common and most dangerous thing we find on first visits. If your parent has a chronic condition — blood pressure, diabetes, thyroid, cardiac — ask specifically about their medication. Not "are you taking your medicines" (the answer will always be yes) but "when did you last refill your blood pressure medication?" or "can you show me your pill box on the camera?"

Medication issues almost always go unreported because parents do not want to worry their children, they have developed their own adjustments (reducing doses, skipping days), or they have run out and the refill requires effort they are not currently managing.

"The parent who manages their own medication for decades can quietly stop, and no one in the family will know for weeks."

The social isolation signal

Perhaps the hardest thing to see from abroad is the gradual retreat from social life. This happens slowly, often without your parent recognising it as significant. Friends stop visiting because they too are less mobile. Going to community events feels like too much effort. Days begin to look the same.

Signs worth noting: specific friends dropping from conversation. No recent stories about going anywhere. Increased focus on television as primary company. Comments about days "passing" rather than being filled.

Why this matters more than most families realise: Social isolation in elderly people is associated with significantly higher risk of depression, cognitive decline, and cardiovascular disease. It is not just a quality of life issue. It is a health issue.

What to do when you notice these signs

Act on it. Not with panic, but with seriousness.

Increased frequency of calling helps short-term but does not change the underlying picture. Asking relatives to "pop in more" is unreliable — they are busy, they are accustomed to your parents, and they may not notice what you are worried about.

What changes the picture is a regular, professional, independent set of eyes in the home. Someone who visits not as family — with all the emotional complexity that brings — but as a trusted professional whose job is to notice, check, and report honestly.
